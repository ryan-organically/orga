The Great Retrieval
The Architect's Guide to Generative Engine Optimization (GEO)

Ryan Scanlon - January 2026

What You're Reading...

Google used to give you ten blue links. Now it gives you an AI-written answer and maybe a couple of citations. ChatGPT, Perplexity, Claude, Gemini. They're all doing the same thing. Your customers ask a question, and an AI answers it, sometimes using your content, usually not.

Generative Engine Optimization (GEO) is how you make sure the AI picks your content as the source. Here's the history, the data, what actually ranks, and how to execute.

Module 1: The Historical & Technical Evolution

From "Ten Blue Links" to "AI Answers"

Google didn't wake up one day and decide to replace links with AI answers. It happened in stages, each one making Google better at understanding what you mean rather than what you type.

2013: Hummingbird

Hummingbird was the first real move away from keyword matching. Google started caring about the meaning behind a query, not the exact words. Amit Singhal (Google's search chief at the time) called it the most significant change since 2001.

The practical effect: a page could rank without containing the precise search terms, as long as it actually answered the question. Keyword stuffing started dying here.

2015: RankBrain

RankBrain pushed things further by mapping queries and pages into vector space. It was a machine-learning system that let Google handle queries it had literally never seen before. Unfamiliar term? RankBrain would map it to a vector and find related concepts.

Google said RankBrain became the third-most important ranking factor after content and links. Intent understanding wasn't a nice-to-have anymore. It was core infrastructure.

2019: BERT

BERT (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers) was the big one. It let Google understand context by looking at words around each word in a sentence. Longer, conversational queries suddenly worked way better.

Simple example: "travelers from Brazil to USA" vs "from USA to Brazil" finally returned different results. BERT stopped treating queries as a bag of words and started reading them more like a human would.

2021: MUM

MUM (Multitask Unified Model) is 1,000x more powerful than BERT, per Google's own claim. It's multimodal (text and images) and multilingual (75 languages).

Google's demo query: "I've hiked Mt. Adams and now want to hike Mt. Fuji next fall, what should I do to prepare?" MUM could understand you're comparing two mountains, that "prepare" means gear and fitness, and pull from Japanese-language sources about Mt. Fuji's fall conditions. That's a completely different kind of search engine.

2023: SGE (Search Generative Experience)

GPT-4 changed user expectations overnight. People started expecting direct answers. Google responded with SGE, an AI answer feature built right into search results.

Ask something complex and Google's AI compiles a conversational answer with cited sources and follow-up questions. It carries context between queries, so search becomes a conversation.

By late 2023 and into 2024, AI answers were appearing on at least 13% of queries (billions of searches per month) and climbing.

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)

Here's what's actually happening under the hood. The AI doesn't rely on its training data alone. It uses Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) to fetch live information from the web before writing an answer.

Step one: retrieve documents that match the query. Step two: synthesize those documents into an answer. Two jobs, one system. This matters because:

  • Allows the model to incorporate up-to-date or domain-specific information
  • Grounds the generation in actual sources
  • Dramatically reduces hallucinations and increases factual accuracy
  • Enables the AI to cite its sources like footnotes

Vector Embeddings and Semantic Proximity

This is where it gets interesting. Modern retrieval runs on vector search. Both the query and your content get converted into numeric representations (embeddings), and the system finds matches based on meaning, not keywords.

Think of it like the Dewey Decimal system. Every book gets a number based on its topic, so related books sit near each other. Vector embeddings do the same thing, they give each piece of content a numerical "address" based on what it means.

This kills the old model of keyword matching. Semantic proximity (how close your content's meaning is to the query) matters more than whether your page contains the exact search terms.

The optimization game shifts from sprinkling exact keywords to covering the entities and context around a topic well enough that the AI considers your content a match.

The Context Window

LLMs can only "read" a fixed number of tokens at a time. That's the context window. GPT-4 ranges from 8K to 32K tokens. The AI can't ingest the entire web for every query. It has to pick what goes in.

In a RAG setup, the system grabs the most relevant text chunks via vector search and feeds them into the model's prompt. If your content doesn't make it into that window, it doesn't exist as far as the AI is concerned.

This is why structure matters so much. If your page gets retrieved but the answer is buried in a 2,000-word wall of text, the model might miss it. If the answer sits in a clean bullet list or a concise paragraph, the AI grabs it with confidence.

Module 2: The Data & Nomenclature

The Rise of GEO in Industry Discourse

Everyone's calling it GEO now, which feels a bit like renaming SEO to SRO in 2010. But the term stuck, and it's already bigger than AEO (Answer Engine Optimization), the last acronym the industry tried to make happen.

Our keyword data: 5,000 monthly U.S. searches for "Generative Engine Optimization" vs. 2,200 for "Answer Engine Optimization." The voice-search era vocabulary is fading. AI-era vocabulary is replacing it.

By late 2025, Wired reported GEO consulting was already an $850 million industry. Startups are popping up everywhere selling "AI visibility" platforms.

The Zero-Click Paradigm

Look, if you want one number that captures this whole shift, it's zero-click searches. That's when the user never clicks through to any website because Google already answered the question (or the user refined their query and moved on).

Over half of all search queries no longer send traffic to the open web. People find what they need on the results page itself, or they give up and try a different query.

If your business model depends on organic search traffic staying flat, you need a new model. We're in a period of secular decline for traditional search clicks.

Corroborating Data Points

  • 95% of B2B buyers plan to use generative AI tools in researching purchases (Forrester)

Impressions and clicks aren't enough anymore. You need to track visibility within AI answers and share of voice in zero-click results. If you're not measuring that, you're flying blind.

Module 3: The Ranking Factors of Generative Engines

Here's the question that matters: what makes an AI cite your content instead of someone else's? AI engines don't rank pages the way Google does. They select sources and synthesize them. Three factors drive that selection: Information Gain, Citation Authority, and Entity Salience.

Information Gain: Rewarding New Information

Google has a 2024 patent called "Contextual Estimation of Link Information Gain." It assigns an information gain score to pages based on how much new stuff they add beyond what the user has already seen.

It's not about having the most information. It's about having the additional piece. If Source A covers points 1, 2, 3, the AI wants Source B for point 4. The missing piece gets the citation.

When Google's AI synthesizes an answer it typically cites about five different sources, and "the content that gets cited is the content that contributes something new. The rest gets absorbed into the synthesis without attribution."

This is actually good news for smaller brands. You'll never outrank Wikipedia in traditional SEO. But publish a study with original data, and Google's AI might cite your site because Wikipedia doesn't have that data point.

Citation Authority: How AI Chooses Which Source to Cite

Traditional Google ranking runs on backlinks. AI answers don't work that way. The AI isn't listing 10 links ordered by PageRank. It cites 2-3 sources inside the answer itself.

Here's what's interesting: SGE is more willing to quote Reddit and Quora than the old organic results ever were. In one dataset from Aug 2024 to mid-2025, the most-cited source in Google's AI Overviews was Reddit (about 2.2% of all citations). User-generated content is winning.

Citation Authority can be thought of as a blend of:

  • Trustworthiness: The source has a good track record or is recognized
  • New information: The source contributes a unique point as per information gain
  • Clarity of content: The source material is extractable and on-topic

One tactic that's working: get mentioned on authoritative third-party sites. You don't even need a link. The AI reads context and picks up on the sentiment around that mention.

Entity Salience and the Knowledge Graph

Google has used entities (people, places, organizations) in ranking for years. With generative AI, entity recognition matters even more.

Google's Natural Language API assigns a "salience score" to every entity in a text, measuring how central it is. The Knowledge Graph assigns confidence levels to facts about those entities.

For brands, this means you need to exist as a recognized entity. Knowledge Panel, Wikipedia page, schema markup declaring who you are. When the AI encounters your brand, it should already know what you are and why you matter.

Brand mentions without links matter more than ever. Authority in the narrative sense (what the AI reads about you) is catching up to authority in the PageRank sense.

Traditional SEO was about getting to Position 1. GEO is about being the source the AI trusts enough to cite. Your KPI isn't clicks alone anymore. It's mentions in AI outputs.

Module 4: The Organic Retrieval Framework (Tactical Execution)

So how do you actually do this? The "Organic Retrieval Framework" is our playbook: a set of tactics that make your content easy for AI to consume and hard for it to ignore.

1. Use Schema Markup That AI Can Parse

Structured data used to be about winning Rich Snippets. Now it's how you tell an AI exactly what your content is. When an LLM reads your page, schema markup gives it explicit context it can't get from prose alone.

Schema types particularly useful for GEO:

  • FAQPage schema: Encapsulates a list of question-answer pairs which an AI can directly use to answer similar questions
  • HowTo schema: Breaks content into discrete steps, aligning with how AI likes bite-sized, structured info
  • ClaimReview schema: Tells AI exactly what claim is being evaluated and the verdict (true/false/context)
  • Speakable schema: Flags the most important 1-2 sentence summary, which could be what an AI chooses to present as the answer text
A study found that pages with proper structured data were 36% more likely to appear in AI-generated results.

2. Write in the "Inverse Pyramid" and S-V-O Style

Journalism's inverted pyramid (lead with the conclusion, then elaborate) turns out to be exactly what AI systems want to read.

When an LLM scans a passage, it's looking for a direct answer or a key fact. If your first sentence answers the who/what/when/where/why, the AI doesn't have to dig for it.

Subject-Verb-Object (S-V-O) matters too. Long dependent clauses and ambiguous pronoun references confuse LLMs. Clear, declarative sentences ("Google updated its algorithm in 2024") are easy for the AI to extract and cite.

Practical tips:

  • Imagine each sentence could be on a flashcard. If that one sentence alone could answer a question, it's a good candidate for extraction
  • Use bullet points or numbered lists for multi-part answers
  • Use bold or italics to highlight key points
  • Avoid overly flowery language or idioms that an AI might misinterpret

3. Optimize Content Elements That AI Is Likely to Cite

When an AI cites a source, it doesn't cite the whole page. It grabs a snippet or a stat. So design your content to have extractable nuggets.

Statistics are the easiest win. Put them in tables or bulleted lists. An AI scanning your page will find a clean table of stats instantly.

Expert quotes work well too. ChatGPT, Perplexity, and Claude all sometimes include quoted sentences with attribution to add credibility. Use blockquotes or quotation marks with the speaker's name.

Headings and subheadings can become the answer text directly. A logical heading hierarchy means any AI-generated outline could mirror yours.

Address common questions explicitly. Phrase an H3 as a question and follow it with a direct answer. If someone asks that exact question to ChatGPT, your text is a perfect match.

Consider adding a summary or key takeaways section. A concise summary can serve as the chunk that AI grabs.

4. "Liquid Content" - Format for Easy Consumption

Liquid content means content that pours easily into any container, including an AI answer box. In practice: lists, tables, definition lists, and short paragraphs with one clear idea each.

Key formatting principles:

  • Use anchor links or IDs for key sections so Google can link to specific paragraphs
  • Make sure your HTML is clean and semantic (use proper heading tags, list markup)
  • Give your images descriptive alt text (the AI might read that when describing something)
  • Ensure your page loads fast and without login walls or pop-ups

Wired put it well: "chatbots tend to favor information presented in simple, structured formats, like bulleted lists and FAQ pages." Treat that as a directive.

What Does an Optimized Page for GEO Look Like?

Say you're writing a blog post on "The Future of Electric Vehicles in 2025." A GEO-optimized version would:

  • Start with a brief summary paragraph hitting the key forecast
  • Use schema markup to identify itself as an Article with author credentials
  • Include an FAQ section with clear Q&A pairs
  • Bold or list important stats: "Key Statistic: 45% of new cars sold in 2025 will be electric"
  • Have a table comparing EV adoption by region (with proper table tags and captions)
  • Include a quote from a notable analyst that the AI could directly quote
  • End with a "Key Takeaways" list summarizing 3-5 big points
  • Have all images with descriptive captions
  • Be published on a site that has entity authority on the topic

That page is built to be pulled apart by AI. Every section is an extractable answer.

Final Thoughts

Structured data, AI-oriented writing, extractable content, fluid formatting. That's the framework. Do those four things and you become the content that teaches the AI.

GEO is happening whether you're ready or not. The playing field is leveling (AI can pull a gem from a 500-visitor site as easily as from a Fortune 500 domain) and tilting at the same time (brands that invest in GEO early will own the answers).

Whether the user clicks or not, your information should be part of the answer. That's the new bar. Let's talk strategy.
