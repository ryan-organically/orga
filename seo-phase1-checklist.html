The SEO Foundation Checklist Every Website Needs

Published April 1, 2026 · 6 min read

You can pour money into ads, crank out blog posts, and redesign your site every six months — but if the foundation isn't right, none of it sticks. This is the checklist we run before we touch anything else. Think of it as the pre-flight inspection before takeoff.

We're not going to walk you through every technical detail here. That's what we do on our end. But you should at least know what to look for — and what to ask your current team about.

Does Your Site Actually Build Without Errors?

This sounds obvious. It's not. Plenty of sites have broken pages, failed builds, or deploy errors that nobody ever checks. If your site throws errors during deployment, Google might be indexing broken pages — or skipping them entirely.

If you're not sure, ask your developer: "When was the last clean deploy?" If they hesitate, you have your answer.

Title Tags and Meta Descriptions

Every page on your site needs a unique title tag and a unique meta description. Not the same one copy-pasted across every page. Not blank. Not "Home" or "Page 1."

Your title tag is the single most important on-page SEO element. It's what shows up in the search results. If every page on your site says the same thing, Google doesn't know which page to show — so it might not show any of them.

Quick check: Google site:yourdomain.com and scan the results. Are the titles distinct and descriptive? Or do they all blur together?

Schema Markup

Schema is structured data that tells search engines exactly what your page is about — your business name, address, services, FAQs, reviews. Without it, you're leaving rich results on the table: star ratings, business hours, FAQ dropdowns, all the stuff that makes certain listings pop in Google.

Most sites don't have it. The ones that do usually only have it on the homepage. Every key page — services, blog posts, FAQ sections — should have validated schema.

Canonical Tags

If Google finds the same content at two different URLs (which happens more than you'd think), it has to pick one. Canonical tags tell Google which version is the "real" one. Without them, you risk splitting your SEO value across duplicate pages and diluting your rankings.

This is one of those invisible things that most business owners never think about, but it can quietly tank your search performance.

Sitemap and Robots.txt

Your sitemap is the roadmap you hand to Google. It lists every page you want indexed. Your robots.txt file tells Google what to ignore. Both need to exist, both need to be correct, and both need to be accessible.

You'd be surprised how many sites accidentally block important pages in robots.txt or have a sitemap that hasn't been updated in two years.

Image Optimization

Images are usually the heaviest thing on your site. If they're not compressed, not in a modern format, and not lazy-loaded, they're slowing everything down. And if they don't have descriptive alt text, Google has no idea what they are.

This is basic stuff, but it's skipped constantly — especially when someone bulk-uploads photos without touching the filenames or alt attributes.

Core Web Vitals

Google measures three things about your site's user experience: how fast the main content loads (LCP), how quickly the page responds to interaction (INP), and how much the layout shifts around while loading (CLS). These aren't vanity metrics — they're ranking factors.

Run your site through Google's PageSpeed Insights. If you're failing any of these, it's costing you visibility.

Google Search Console and Business Profile

Search Console is how Google talks to you. It tells you what queries you're showing up for, which pages have errors, and whether your sitemap was accepted. If you haven't set it up, you're flying blind.

And if you're a local business, your Google Business Profile needs to be claimed, verified, and completely filled out. Your name, address, and phone number need to match what's on your website and every other listing you have online. Inconsistencies confuse Google and hurt your local rankings.

Open Graph Tags

When someone shares your page on LinkedIn, Facebook, or in a text message, Open Graph tags control what shows up — the title, description, and image. Without them, the preview is either blank or pulls something random from your page. It's a small detail, but it affects how professional your brand looks when people share your content.

That's the Baseline

This isn't the whole game — it's just the starting line. But you'd be surprised how many businesses skip straight to content strategy, link building, or paid ads without nailing these fundamentals first. It's like putting premium gas in a car with a cracked engine block.

Get the foundation right, and everything you build on top of it works harder.
